## Naming sessions and classes

A name you write today is a search query in three months. Patterns that scale:

-   **Session:** `{Student}, {Date}, {Focus}` — "Maya, 2026-05-04, guard retention".
-   **Class:** `{Day}, {Topic}, {Level}` — "Mon, scissor sweeps, intermediate".
-   Be consistent across coaches — a shared convention compounds.

## Tagging conventions

Tags are free-form text; they only become useful with team discipline. Pick one style and stick to it. Common starting points for BJJ:

-   **Position:** `guard`, `mount`, `side-control`, `back`.
-   **Action:** `sweep`, `pass`, `escape`, `submission`.
-   **Technique:** `armbar`, `triangle`, `kimura` — get specific.
-   **Phase:** `fundamentals`, `comp-prep`, `rehab`.

Don't mix styles in one tag (no `guardpass`, `guard-pass`, and `Guard Pass` for the same thing). Search treats them as separate.

## Class structure that gets watched

A 60-minute class recording, posted untouched, has a brutally low completion rate. What works:

-   **Upload the full class** so it exists in the archive.
-   **Pull a 2–3 minute recap clip** of the most important moment — a sweep variation, a key correction, a sparring exchange worth dissecting. The recap gets watched 5–10× more often than the full recording.
-   Tag the clip with the same vocabulary as the source session.

Recap clip workflow: [Watching and reviewing → Recap clips](/playback).

## Notes that students act on

Three habits that turn passive watching into actual change:

-   **Question, not statement:** "What's happening with your right hand here?" beats "Your right hand is wrong." Students engage with questions.
-   **Reference earlier work:** @mention a previous session note when the same issue recurs. The pattern becomes visible.
-   **One thing per note:** timestamped notes are scannable. Don't write paragraphs — write three short notes at three moments instead.

## Review requests

Encourage students to submit short clips (under 2 minutes) with one specific question. Long unfocused requests sit in your queue; short specific ones get fast responses and create a virtuous loop.

## AI review

AI review (powered by Twelve Labs) surfaces moments worth attention. Treat it as a draft, not a verdict — edit, keep, or delete each suggestion. Good fits:

-   Long sparring footage where you want to skim for technique-relevant moments before writing notes manually.
-   Pulling potential recap clips from a multi-pair sparring session.

Bad fits: cold-reading footage for tactical decisions specific to your gym's curriculum. The model doesn't know your house techniques.

## Voice dictation

For long review sessions where typing slows you down: click the mic icon in the note editor. Audio is transcribed in real time by Deepgram and appears as you talk. Edit before saving. Microphone access required; consent prompt appears the first time.

## Progress reviews

Run a quick pass on the [progress dashboard](/progress) weekly — who watched what, who hasn't. Reach out directly to students who haven't watched anything for two weeks. The intervention is small; the retention impact compounds.

## Multi-coach gyms

Each coach has their own student roster, but classes are visible to everyone. Conventions worth aligning on at a team level:

-   Tag vocabulary (above).
-   Who owns class recordings (one person, or the coach who taught).
-   How to handle review requests when the student picked another coach.
-   Whether assistant coaches add notes to a head coach's sessions (most do — clarify expectations).

## Tournament footage

If you record competition footage for review:

-   Upload to a **session** assigned to the competitor — it's their performance, even if you debrief in class later.
-   Mirror the recap-clip pattern: a 30-second clip of the key match moment gets watched; the full match doesn't.
-   Tag with the event name plus your house convention.


<Tip>
  The single highest-impact habit: write at least one note per video. Notes you write are notes students return to. Notes you don't write fade.
</Tip>
